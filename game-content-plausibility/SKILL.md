# Game Content Plausibility

Audit the claim as a prediction, not as a design prompt. Separate “could be made” from “is likely to be made.”

## Establish the proposition

State the narrow claim being tested. Identify its implied game, timeframe, content type, role, setting, and source material. Preserve reference layers and user-supplied clues, but do not silently promote them to facts.

Decompose compound predictions before scoring them. Distinguish claims such as:

- the project exists or remains active;
- the publisher or studio attends an event;
- the title appears during that event window;
- the appearance contains a reveal, trailer, gameplay, demo, date, or release;
- an adjacent showcase, Direct, summit, anniversary, or investor update counts as part of the same prediction.

Attach every confidence label to one explicit proposition. Do not let confirmation of a game, release date, publisher attendance, or neighboring event silently confirm a prediction about a specific showcase appearance.

If an unfamiliar name, place, leak, image, or premise could materially alter the analysis, verify it before building conclusions around it.

## Gather current evidence

Use web search when the question is complex enough that recent information could materially change the conclusion. Search especially for:

- live-service updates, upcoming content, patches, interviews, leaks, datamines, and release patterns;
- current canon, developer statements, rosters, newly introduced locations, or expansion clues;
- claims that may have changed since the model's knowledge baseline;
- unfamiliar terms or user premises whose status is uncertain.

Prefer official and primary sources. Use reputable reporting when primary evidence is unavailable. Do not browse merely to decorate a stable answer; browse when fresh evidence could confirm, weaken, or overturn it. If reliable evidence cannot be found, say so and continue with a labeled best estimate.

Resolve scope before inference. For studios with multiple projects, identify which title, team, production phase, platform, region, event, and timeframe each statement concerns. Do not transfer evidence between projects merely because they share a developer, publisher, franchise presentation, earnings call, or marketing window. Treat “full production,” “pre-production,” “most advanced,” “next,” and “priority” as different claims unless the source equates them.

Classify material as:

1. **Confirmed fact:** official, current, and directly relevant.
2. **Credible signal:** reliable but indirect evidence.
3. **Interpretation:** a reasonable connection among known clues.
4. **Rumor or leak:** unconfirmed information with explicit provenance and reliability limits.
5. **Speculation:** a possibility not independently supported.

Never treat search-result absence as proof that something does not exist. Never treat mechanical designability as evidence of developer intent.

## Test competing explanations

For each important clue, compare at least one credible alternative explanation. Ask whether the clue more naturally indicates:

- the proposed playable content;
- an NPC, boss, cosmetic, minigame, event, location, or narrative reference;
- reused franchise imagery without mechanical implications;
- coincidence or community overinterpretation.

Search for disconfirming evidence and constraints, not only supporting clues.

Test event boundaries. Publisher floor presence supports some form of participation, not every title on its slate. A showcase held near TGS, Gamescom, Summer Game Fest, an anniversary, or a platform presentation may be a parallel branch rather than part of the event itself. State the boundary used before judging a hit or miss.

Avoid double-counting correlated clues. If anniversary timing, merchandise, a remaster, social posts, and an event date all plausibly arise from one marketing campaign, treat them as one clue cluster until independent causation is shown. Compare the pattern against the publisher's base rate: recurring showcases, annual DLC, perpetual live-service updates, and habitual event attendance are weaker evidence than unusual deviations.

When wording such as “original,” “new,” “not a legacy job,” or “never seen before” is used to exclude a theory, test how the developer has used that language historically. Distinguish an original job or rules identity from original weapons, motifs, animations, or character inspiration; a newly formalized job may still adapt the combat vocabulary of an earlier character.

For Final Fantasy XIV speculation, account for “Yoshi-P gonna Yoshi-P”: Naoki Yoshida's shirts, jokes, evasive wording, and staged hints may be truthful, indirect, multi-layered, or deliberate misdirection. Treat them as clue objects that require precedent and competing interpretations, not as literal confirmations or clean exclusions. Preserve the playful/trolling layer without using it to make the theory unfalsifiable.

Apply the same general principle to any speaker or studio: jokes, technically true wording, selective disclosure, staged ambiguity, and expectation management are evidence about communication strategy, not automatic evidence for the favored theory. Use documented historical behavior to widen or narrow uncertainty; do not invent an “excuse layer” that protects a prediction from falsification.

## Run feasibility passes

Evaluate only the layers relevant to the claim:

- **Concept fit:** identity, role, silhouette, and overlap with existing content.
- **Canon and lore fit:** how it can enter the setting and what continuity problems remain.
- **Gameplay fit:** whether the source fantasy survives the game's actual combat and interface model.
- **Production fit:** animation, assets, balance, encounter support, technical cost, and opportunity cost.
- **Release-pattern fit:** role gaps, expansion themes, precedent, timing, and marketing value.
- **Audience fit:** recognition, regional appeal, controversy, and expectation risk.

Do not solve a lore gap by inventing unsupported lore and then count that invention as evidence. Label adaptations as possible implementation paths only.

## Use a second design skill only when needed

Keep this skill as the owning analysis layer. Invoke a design skill such as Forge RPG Design only when the user also asks how the content could mechanically work, requests a prototype, or when a bounded mechanics pass is necessary to test feasibility.

When using a design skill second:

1. Pass it the verified anchors, exclusions, and system constraints.
2. Request only the minimum design needed to test viability.
3. Return to this skill for probability and evidence judgments.
4. Do not let an elegant design raise the likelihood score without external evidence.

## Calibrate the verdict

Give separate judgments for:

- **Design feasibility:** could competent developers make it work?
- **Evidence strength:** how strongly do current clues point toward it?
- **Actual likelihood:** is the developer likely to choose it over alternatives?
- **Confidence:** how stable is the assessment given source quality and missing information?

Explain what evidence would materially raise or lower the likelihood. Use numeric ratings only when their basis is stated; avoid false precision.

Lead with the verdict. Then give the decisive evidence, strongest alternative explanation, major obstacles, most credible implementation path, and uncertainty. Cite current sources near the claims they support.
