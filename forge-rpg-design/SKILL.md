# Forge RPG Design

Turn a fantasy into a rules object by eliminating directions that fail its scene, scope, or system constraints. Preserve the user's voice and imagery; make the rules support them.

## Establish the source of truth

1. Read the supplied class, subsystem, veil, image, or prior draft before proposing changes.
2. Extract binding decisions, exclusions, terminology, scaling conventions, and unresolved questions.
3. Separate canon from suggestion. Do not silently reopen settled decisions.
4. When prior work is referenced but unavailable, retrieve it when possible. Ask for the missing artifact only when its absence would materially change the design.

## Resolve inheritance before redesign

Before auditing a class or option set, map its inheritance:

1. Identify canonical parent systems, shared chassis features, sibling-class imports, adapters, and genuinely local mechanics.
2. Import finalized shared rules verbatim. Change only names or interface terms that must change; do not rebalance or paraphrase each copy independently.
3. Audit related classes by difference: shared chassis, shared selections, exclusive spine, alternate engines, and unique progression.
4. When an adapter summarizes another subsystem, inspect the owning subsystem before repairing the adapter. The parent's profile, capacities, refresh rules, and terminology outrank the local summary.
5. If the parent system is missing or unfinished, classify the adapter as **reserve** rather than inventing a provisional interface that may later conflict.
6. Treat repeated copies of canonical subsystems as a maintenance risk. Recommend one source of truth when duplication has already caused version drift.

For a fork, do not infer its identity from the loudest inherited feature. State the parent chassis, remove unchanged inheritance from the identity test, then find the exclusive mechanic that changes the recurring scene. Estimate and audit the fork by its differences, even when copied material occupies most of the document.

## Reverse-engineer complex catalogues interactively

Before applying judgment compression to a long list of brands, souls, companions, veils, talents, or modules, determine whether the entries are variants of one template or individually authored adaptations.

1. Sample entries from different apparent families. Separate shared chassis, transferable techniques, body or state features, unique loops, roster roles, and imported subsystems.
2. Classify reference intent as **direct** (one anchor), **composite** (several deliberate layers or a range), or **generic** (the creature or archetype itself). Do not hunt for a secret source inside every generic option.
3. Present one compact provisional reading of the next coherent entry or family: what appears shared, what appears unique, and the inferred play loop.
4. Invite correction of the decisive assumption, not approval of every talent. Treat the user's correction as source-of-truth design evidence.
5. Convert the correction into the smallest reusable rule that applies forward. Prefer an intuitive semantic invariant such as “techniques transfer; bodies do not” over tagging or caveating hundreds of entries.
6. Reapply the new rule to later entries without asking the same category of question again. Ask again only for a genuine exception, conflicting anchor, or missing owner.
7. Stop the reverse pass when the remaining entries can be classified safely. If questions multiply instead of shrinking uncertainty, repair the governing model before continuing.

Repeated mechanics may encode deployment states rather than lazy duplication. Before merging them, test whether they support distinct states such as manifested companion, ensemble synergy, transformed equipment, stored power, alternate loadout, or merged form. Also test roster identity: an option may be distinct because it introduces the first beast, retainer, rival, male-coded companion, commander, mount, or subsystem specialist even when part of its talent list is shared.

Do not confuse a summarized or reconstructed document with a complete source. Phrases such as “full rules as in the original” are missing-source markers, not permission to recreate the absent text. Preserve the dependency, revise what is present, and identify the completion gate.

## Apply the reference steering layer

Treat a named person, wrestler, myth, joke, pun, media or historical reference, brand-like title, or layered wordplay as design instruction rather than decorative flavor.

Before naming the soul and scene, extract and privately record:

1. **Reference anchor:** the exact person, work, joke, phrase, archetype, or source being invoked.
2. **Intended meaning:** the qualities that matter to this design. Do not assume the reference is literal.
3. **Mechanical translation:** the mechanics, action patterns, progression, presentation, and player fantasy that express those qualities.
4. **Tonal translation:** the implied comedy, menace, spectacle, elegance, brutality, parody, nostalgia, camp, or other tone.
5. **Avoided drift:** the obvious but incorrect readings that would pull the design away from the intended concept.
6. **Reference priority:** when the visual description, title, pun, and named reference diverge, follow the user's explicitly explained meaning.

Preserve simultaneous reference layers when they reinforce rather than contradict one another. Use the resulting mechanical and tonal translations to shape the soul, scene, spine, and candidate gates. Treat avoided drift as binding exclusions. Publish this analysis only when it helps verify direction or preserve a layered concept for future revision.

Before cutting an apparently weak, redundant, or strange option, test whether its intended scene is civilian, narrative, ritual, social, comedic, devotional, or exploratory rather than combat-facing. A harvest dance, disciplinary fan bonk, inspection form, travel ward, or community blessing can be successful without competing on combat throughput. Preserve that job and repair its interface instead of converting every option into damage, defense, or condition removal.

Before estimating scope or answering technically, run a reference-intent check. Do not inflate a compact parody, crossover, meme, or joke prototype into the full scope of every named property. When the likely intent is clear but not certain, state the narrow interpretation in one sentence—such as “Assuming you mean a small joke prototype…”—then answer under that assumption. Ask a focused question only when the alternate readings would materially change the result.

Examples:

- **MONSOON INDUSTRIES: GORILLA** anchors Gorilla Monsoon, heavyweight wrestling, and gorilla/King Kong imagery. Translate immense presence, grappling dominance, forced movement, close-range spectacle, and overwhelming impact into bull rushes, grapples, sunders, momentum attacks, movement into displaced spaces, and temporary overdrive. Avoid generic ape transformation, precision martial arts, stealth cyberware, or a bare Strength bonus without battlefield control.
- **BRONSON ARMS** anchors Bear Bronson, “bear arms” wordplay, a Second Amendment joke, and cybernetic bear limbs. Translate wrestler-like crushing power, bear-hug control, heavy protection, and weaponized comedy. Avoid generic claws, literal firearm-replacement arms, or ordinary animal transformation.
- **Negative pattern—Mario throwing Hadoukens in World 1-1:** detect the parody/crossover framing before estimating production. Do not assume a full Mario 64 recreation or ignore the joke and return only a schedule. Unless context contradicts it, narrow the premise to a small joke prototype, acknowledge that assumption, then give the technical answer.

## Name the soul and scene

Write two private working statements:

- **Soul:** one sentence naming the silhouette, motion, material identity, attitude, and promise.
- **Scene:** a short sequence of what the player should repeatedly do at the table.

Prefer a playable sequence such as `observe -> plan -> misdirect -> enter -> strike -> exit` over a genre label such as “ninja.” If the concept remains generic, ask for one reference image, character, or media anchor before inventing more mechanics.

Do not publish these statements unless they help the user choose or verify a direction.

## Select the owning rules layer

Choose the smallest layer that genuinely owns the behavior:

- **Class:** a full progression and identity spine.
- **Archetype:** a ruleset that replaces or redirects an existing chassis.
- **Alternate class feature:** a bounded exchange for one feature or feature chain.
- **Subsystem:** reusable rules with its own vocabulary, scaling, and safety boundaries.
- **Veil or item-like module:** a shaped/equipped expression with slots, binds, investment, or configuration.
- **Talent/art/module:** one selectable upgrade within an existing spine.
- **Adapter:** a narrow bridge importing an independent subsystem into another chassis.

Reject a direction when it solves the fantasy at the wrong layer. Do not turn a class feature into a veil, a loadout into a companion, or an adapter into ownership of the imported subsystem.

## Forge the spine

1. Identify the one mechanic that makes the scene recur.
2. Make surrounding options deepen, redirect, or cash out that mechanic.
3. Define the minimum invariants: action type, resource owner, duration, target, scaling source, refresh rule, and interaction boundary.
4. Add explicit negative rules where the fantasy could imply unintended economies, extra actors, turns, summons, refreshes, slots, or stacking.
5. Build progression from new decisions or scene changes, not numeric inflation alone.

If no candidate can carry the full scene, stop expanding the option list and repair the spine.

## Eliminate weak directions

Generate only a small candidate set. Compare candidates directly and cut aggressively using these gates:

1. **Scene gate:** Does it create the intended play sequence, or only wear the concept's costume?
2. **Soul gate:** Can the player see and feel it manifest? Does its motion and material identity match the anchor?
3. **Scope gate:** Does it belong to this rules layer and respect settled ownership?
4. **Spine gate:** Does it reinforce the core loop rather than establish a second competing engine?
5. **Distinctness gate:** Is it meaningfully different from an existing option, not a renamed bonus?
6. **Choice gate:** Does it create a real tactical or build decision rather than an automatic pick or tax?
7. **Economy gate:** Does it preserve actions, resources, slots, initiative, and refresh continuity?
8. **Progression gate:** Does it scale from the correct level/HD/stat and remain relevant without bypassing prerequisites?
9. **Interface gate:** Are DCs, descriptors, binds, capacities, stacking, and cross-system hooks explicit?
10. **Text gate:** Can it be stated cleanly without explaining away contradictions?

Classify each serious candidate as **keep**, **merge**, **reserve**, or **kill**. Give the decisive reason in one line. Do not preserve a weak option merely to fill a count.

Read [references/decision-patterns.md](references/decision-patterns.md) when applying these gates to a complex revision or when a concrete precedent would help.

## Use guided elimination for ambiguity

When a genuine fork remains, ask one focused question with two or three mutually exclusive options. Frame each option around what changes in play, not around names. Put the strongest inferred direction first. Avoid broad brainstorming questions and long menus.

Do not ask when existing anchors, exclusions, or chassis rules already decide the issue.

## Run the mechanics pass

After the fantasy and structure survive elimination, verify:

- action economy and timing;
- resource ownership, expenditure, and refresh continuity;
- targets, range, duration, frequency, and saving throws;
- scaling source and multiclass/cross-training behavior;
- prerequisites, mandatory pacing, and dead-level pressure;
- slots, binds, descriptors, investment caps, and reconfiguration timing;
- stacking, bonus types, caps, and unintended feedback loops;
- independent-creature, extra-turn, item-economy, and summon implications;
- compatibility with the source chassis and established terminology.

For every resource or loadout, distinguish four separate questions: what is known or installed, how much can be prepared or held, what is spent on use, and what refreshes it. Do not collapse batch count, items per batch, slot capacity, daily uses, active selections, and known options into one ambiguous resource.

Check that every named progression feature pays out when gained. Permission to advance after the normal level range, future compatibility, or an unresolved placeholder is not by itself an immediate capstone or level feature.

Prefer a narrow safety sentence over repeated caveats in every option.

## Write the rules without sanding off the soul

Preserve sentence rhythm, names, silhouette, motion, and material imagery. Correct typos, confusing references, broken tables, and mechanical ambiguity. Do not sterilize evocative text merely to sound official.

Structure the result in this order when useful:

1. manifestation or fantasy sentence;
2. base rule;
3. scaling or investment;
4. bind/upgrade/advanced expression;
5. compatibility or safety clause.

## Deliver a revision

Lead with the surviving design and usable rules text. Then provide only the decision trail that helps future revision:

- settled scope and spine;
- options kept, merged, reserved, or killed;
- unresolved dependency or required reference;
- compact abuse/compatibility notes.

Avoid padding the result with rejected brainstorms. If the user asks only for prose cleanup, do not redesign the mechanics.

When the user authorizes broad judgment, switch to **judgment compression**: audit a coherent family of talents, beads, dances, modules, or similar options in one pass. Preserve distinct options and report only material changes, merges or removals, reserved dependencies, compatibility risks, and genuine unresolved forks. Do not continue asking for approval on every micro-decision after that authorization.
